The situation

Three hundred candidates, one week, no budget.

A placement cell screening a graduating class. A training institute certifying a cohort. A sixty-person company that needs a shortlist by Friday. All three need the same four things: a different paper for every candidate, a clock nobody can edit, some idea of who was looking at another window, and a spreadsheet at the end.

A form tool gives you none of them. An assessment platform gives you all four, charges by the candidate, and keeps the data on its servers.

What changes

A form shows everyone the same paper.

Pariksha draws each candidate's paper from a pool you tag and filter, or hands out fixed sets A, B and C round-robin by registration order, so neighbours never sit the same questions. Options shuffle underneath, except the ones you pin.

The answer key never leaves the server. The clock is the server's, so changing the computer's time does nothing, and an answer arriving thirty seconds late is refused. A field marked unique refuses a second registration with the same roll number.

What you ask

Twelve types. Five ways to score them.

Matching, multi-matching, ordering, numeric with tolerance, fill in the blank with regex — alongside the four every form tool has. Markdown, LaTeX, code blocks and images in each one.

All-or-nothing, partial credit, negative marking, ordering by position, rubrics. Change a key after the exam and regrade every attempt, with one audit entry.

What you get back

One row per candidate, in a file you already know.

A column for every section and every registration field, with rank, percent, pass or fail against your pass mark, and the integrity flag. UTF-8 with a BOM, so non-Latin names open correctly in Excel.

You never write JSON. The format exists so that you can — and so nothing you build is trapped.

Integrity

No video. No audio. No verdicts.

Strict proctoring takes a 320×240 snapshot about every sixty seconds, counts faces on the device itself, and stores no biometric template. Nothing is streamed. Nothing is recorded. Snapshots sit in your own private bucket behind links that expire in ten minutes.

Tab switches, fullscreen exits and copy attempts are logged, and the candidate sees a counted warning rather than a silent mark against them. At the end Pariksha marks the attempt clean, review or high risk — and stops.

Browser checks deter and record. They cannot prove: a second phone is invisible to every one of them, and our own documentation says so. That is precisely why no machine here is allowed to reach a verdict.

An estimate with published arithmetic. The 500-candidate figure is a design target; the load test that would prove it (AC-29) is a Phase 12 gate and has not been run. There is no Pariksha billing of any kind and no hosted service to bill for.

Where it stands

What exists today, and what lands with v0.1.

  • The question and test formats, and their JSON Schemas
  • All 12 question types, graded and unit-tested
  • All 5 scoring modes, with applicable modes enforced per type
  • Pool draws, set assignment, seeded shuffling
  • Cross-field import validation — one bad question imports nothing
  • A 17-question worked example covering all 12 types
  • This website
  • The authoring UI and the test builder
  • The candidate runtime
  • Proctoring — three profiles, Off, Standard and Strict
  • Results — CSV, live monitor, grading queue, item analysis
  • The API and signed webhooks
  • Organiser sign-in, roles and workspaces

A paid platform you can buy today will do more than Pariksha does today, because the application is not built yet. What is built is the format, the schemas and the scoring engine — and you can run those today.

What you can do today

  1. Write your question bank. The format is published and stable; questions written today import on day one — or into anything else, since the schemas are CC BY 4.0.
  2. Validate it in one command. One npx ajv run. No install, no account.
  3. Read a whole exam. Seventeen questions, three sections, the candidate's screen and the results CSV it produces.
