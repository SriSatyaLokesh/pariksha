Band 3 · The scene — the buyer's situation, in their words · 140 words

It is February. The hall holds eighty. The class is three hundred.

You are the placement cell. Four companies want a screening round before they visit. You have a week, a spreadsheet of roll numbers, a lab with sixty machines, and no budget line for "assessment software".

So you build a Google Form. Then you notice: everyone gets the same paper, and the answer key is sitting in the page you just sent them. The timer is whatever the browser says it is. You cannot tell who opened another tab. Two people register with the same roll number. And on Friday, you rebuild the results by hand because the form has no idea what a section or a pass mark is.

That is the gap Pariksha was written for.

Band 4 · Resolution — each failure above, answered in order · 200 words

A form shows everyone the same paper. An exam should not.

What went wrong with the formWhat Pariksha does
Everyone got the same paperFour questions drawn per candidate from a tagged pool, or fixed sets A/B/C handed out round-robin so neighbours never match
The answer key was in the pageKeys and explanations stay on the server and are never sent to a candidate's browser
The timer was the browser'sThe deadline is the server's. Changing the computer clock does nothing; answers more than 30 seconds late are refused
You could not see a tab switchTab switches, fullscreen exits and copy attempts are logged, and the candidate sees a counted warning
Two people used one roll numberAny registration field can be marked unique; the second attempt is refused on the server
Results had to be rebuilt by handOne CSV row per candidate with a column per section and per form field, rank, pass/fail and the integrity flag

The first three rows are implemented and unit-tested today. The last three are specified and land with v0.1 — see what is live.

Band 5 · The ethic — the band that wins the privacy review · 150 words

Integrity without surveillance: no video, no audio, no verdicts.

Pariksha never records a candidate. Strict proctoring takes a 320×240 snapshot about once a minute, counts faces on the device itself, and stores no biometric template. Snapshots sit in your own bucket behind links that expire in ten minutes.

At the end it marks the attempt clean, review or high risk — and stops. Browser checks deter and record; they cannot prove. A person decides what a flag means.

An estimate with published arithmetic. The load test that would prove it (AC-29) is a Phase 12 gate and has not been run.

Nothing, ever, to Pariksha. There is no Pariksha billing of any kind and no hosted service to bill for. Candidate data lives in your account — no telemetry, no third-party scripts, and a whole test exports as one open JSON file, so nothing you build is trapped.
